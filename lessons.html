01What makes a problem dynamic?Beginner8 min

A load is static if it changes so slowly that inertia can be ignored. It is dynamic when it varies quickly enough that the structure's own mass accelerates, so inertia forces become part of the equilibrium. A useful rule of thumb: if the load changes over a time comparable to, or shorter than, the structure's natural period, treat it dynamically. Wind gusts, machinery, traffic, blast and earthquakes are classic dynamic loads.

Degrees of freedom

The number of degrees of freedom (DOF) is the number of independent displacement coordinates needed to describe the position of all the mass at any instant. Real structures have infinitely many; we idealise them by lumping mass at a few points. A shear building with rigid floors and one lateral displacement per floor has as many DOF as it has storeys.

The four ingredients

Mass \(m\) resists acceleration, stiffness \(k\) resists deformation, damping \(c\) dissipates energy, and excitation \(p(t)\) drives the motion. Writing equilibrium with D'Alembert's principle (inertia, damping and elastic forces balance the applied load) gives the equation of motion of a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) system:

$$m\ddot{u}+c\dot{u}+ku=p(t)$$
Example: counting DOFA 4-storey frame whose floors are rigid in their own plane and whose columns are axially rigid moves only sideways at each floor, so it has 4 DOF. A single-storey water tank on a slender column, with all the mass at the top, has 1 DOF.

02Undamped free vibrationBeginner10 min

Disturb an SDOF system and release it with no external load and no damping. The equation is \(m\ddot u+ku=0\), whose solution is a pure sinusoid at the natural circular frequency \(\omega_n\).

$$u(t)=u_0\cos\omega_n t+\frac{\dot u_0}{\omega_n}\sin\omega_n t,\qquad \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$
$$f_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi},\qquad T_n=\frac{1}{f_n}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_n}$$

The natural frequency depends only on mass and stiffness, so it is a property of the structure and not of how hard you pushed it. A stiffer or lighter structure vibrates faster.

ExampleA tank of mass \(m=20{,}000\) kg sits on columns with total lateral stiffness \(k=2.0\times10^{6}\) N/m. Then \(\omega_n=\sqrt{2\times10^6/20{,}000}=10\) rad/s, \(f_n=10/2\pi=1.59\) Hz and \(T_n=0.628\) s. Doubling the stiffness raises \(\omega_n\) by only \(\sqrt2\), to 14.1 rad/s.
RememberUse consistent units. With mass in tonnes and stiffness in kN/m, \(\sqrt{k/m}\) comes out directly in rad/s.

03Damped free vibrationBeginner12 min

Real structures lose energy through material hysteresis, friction in connections and cracking. We model this with a viscous damper \(c\). The damping is described by the damping ratio relative to the critical value:

$$c_{cr}=2m\omega_n,\qquad \xi=\frac{c}{c_{cr}}$$

If \(\xi\lt1\) (underdamped, the case for essentially all buildings and bridges) the motion oscillates inside a decaying exponential envelope:

$$u(t)=e^{-\xi\omega_n t}\left[u_0\cos\omega_D t+\frac{\dot u_0+\xi\omega_n u_0}{\omega_D}\sin\omega_D t\right],\qquad \omega_D=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$$

At \(\xi=1\) the system returns without oscillating and for \(\xi\gt1\) it creeps back slowly. Damping ratios are typically a few percent, and 5% is the classic default for response spectra. Because damping is so small, \(\omega_D\approx\omega_n\).

Measuring damping: the logarithmic decrement

The ratio of two successive peaks gives \(\xi\) experimentally:

$$\delta=\ln\frac{u_i}{u_{i+1}}=\frac{2\pi\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\approx2\pi\xi$$
ExampleFor the tank in lesson 2 with \(\xi=5\%\): \(c_{cr}=2(20{,}000)(10)=400{,}000\) N·s/m, so \(c=20{,}000\) N·s/m. Then \(\omega_D=10\sqrt{1-0.0025}=9.99\) rad/s and \(\delta=0.315\). Each peak is \(e^{0.315}=1.37\) times smaller than the previous one, and it takes about \(\ln2/\delta\approx2.2\) cycles to halve the amplitude.

04Harmonic loading and resonanceIntermediate14 min

Apply \(p(t)=p_0\sin\bar\omega t\). After the transient dies out, the system vibrates at the forcing frequency with a constant amplitude. Define the frequency ratio \(r=\bar\omega/\omega_n\).

$$u_p(t)=\frac{p_0}{k}\,D\,\sin(\bar\omega t-\theta),\quad D=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\xi r)^2}},\quad \theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{2\xi r}{1-r^2}$$

\(D\) is the dynamic amplification factor, the ratio of the dynamic to the static deflection \(p_0/k\). It tells you everything:

  • \(r\ll1\): slow loading, \(D\approx1\), the response is quasi-static and in phase with the load.
  • \(r\approx1\): resonance, the response is limited only by damping.
  • \(r\gg1\): fast loading, \(D\approx1/r^2\), the mass hardly moves and the response is inertia-controlled and out of phase.
$$D_{r=1}=\frac{1}{2\xi}$$
ExampleThe tank from lesson 2 (\(\omega_n=10\) rad/s, \(\xi=5\%\)) is hit by a rotating machine producing \(p_0=50\) kN at 10 rad/s. Static deflection \(p_0/k=50{,}000/2\times10^6=25\) mm. At resonance \(D=1/(0.1)=10\), so the steady-state amplitude is \(250\) mm, ten times the static value. Detune the machine to \(r=2\): \(D=1/\sqrt{9+0.04}=0.33\), and the amplitude drops to 8.3 mm.
Design takeawayKeep the forcing frequency well away from \(\omega_n\), or add damping if you cannot.

05Arbitrary loading: Duhamel and NewmarkIntermediate15 min

Wind gusts, blasts and impacts are not harmonic. For a linear system, treat the load as a train of tiny impulses and add up the responses (superposition). This gives Duhamel's integral, starting from rest:

$$u(t)=\frac{1}{m\omega_D}\int_0^t p(\tau)\,e^{-\xi\omega_n(t-\tau)}\sin\omega_D(t-\tau)\,d\tau$$
Example: suddenly applied constant loadFor an undamped system with \(p(t)=p_0\) for \(t\ge0\), the integral gives \(u(t)=\frac{p_0}{k}(1-\cos\omega_n t)\). The peak is exactly twice the static deflection, a result worth memorising. Damping reduces it slightly below 2.

Numerical integration

For general loads, nonlinear behaviour or MDOF systems, we step through time. Newmark's method assumes how acceleration varies within a step and uses two parameters \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\):

$$\dot u_{i+1}=\dot u_i+\Delta t\left[(1-\gamma)\ddot u_i+\gamma\ddot u_{i+1}\right],\quad u_{i+1}=u_i+\Delta t\,\dot u_i+\Delta t^2\left[(\tfrac12-\beta)\ddot u_i+\beta\ddot u_{i+1}\right]$$
  • \(\gamma=\tfrac12,\ \beta=\tfrac14\) (average acceleration): unconditionally stable for linear systems and the standard choice.
  • \(\gamma=\tfrac12,\ \beta=\tfrac16\) (linear acceleration): more accurate but only conditionally stable, needing \(\Delta t/T_n\le0.551\).

Stability is not accuracy. Use \(\Delta t\le T_n/10\) (smaller if the load has sharp features) to resolve the response properly.

06Base excitation and response spectraIntermediate14 min

When the ground shakes with acceleration \(\ddot u_g(t)\), the relative displacement \(u\) of the structure obeys the equation of an SDOF system loaded by an effective force \(-m\ddot u_g\):

$$m\ddot u+c\dot u+ku=-m\,\ddot u_g(t)$$

Solving this for one ground motion and many oscillators (a range of \(T_n\), fixed \(\xi\)) and recording the peak of each gives the response spectrum. The spectral displacement is \(S_d\), and the pseudo-acceleration and equivalent static base shear follow:

$$S_a=\omega_n^2\,S_d,\qquad V_b=m\,S_a$$

This turns an earthquake analysis into a simple look-up: find \(T_n\), read \(S_a\) from the design spectrum, and apply a static force.

ExampleFor the tank (\(m=20{,}000\) kg, \(T_n=0.628\) s, \(\omega_n=10\) rad/s) suppose the 5% spectrum gives \(S_d=50\) mm at this period. Then \(S_a=100\times0.05=5\) m/s² (about 0.51 g) and \(V_b=20{,}000\times5=100\) kN. Check: \(k\,S_d=2\times10^6\times0.05=100\) kN ✓.

Vibration isolation

For a machine on a spring-damper mount, the force transmitted to the floor is \(TR\cdot p_0\) with

$$TR=D\sqrt{1+(2\xi r)^2}$$

Isolation (\(TR\lt1\)) only occurs when \(r\gt\sqrt2\). Below that, a spring mount makes things worse.

07MDOF systems and modal analysisAdvanced18 min

For \(N\) degrees of freedom the equation of motion becomes a matrix equation:

$$\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{p}(t)$$

Free vibration without damping assumes motion of the form \(\mathbf u=\boldsymbol\phi\sin\omega t\), leading to the generalised eigenvalue problem:

$$(\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)\boldsymbol\phi=\mathbf 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\det(\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)=0$$

There are \(N\) natural frequencies \(\omega_1\lt\omega_2\lt\dots\) and \(N\) mode shapes \(\boldsymbol\phi_n\). The key property is orthogonality: \(\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_j=0\) and \(\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi_j=0\) for \(i\ne j\). This is what allows the coupled equations to be decoupled in the next lesson.

Example: 2-storey shear buildingEqual masses \(m\) and equal storey stiffnesses \(k\). Then \(\mathbf M=m\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf K=k\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\). With \(\lambda=\omega^2m/k\), the characteristic equation is \(\lambda^2-3\lambda+1=0\), so \(\lambda=(3\mp\sqrt5)/2=0.382,\ 2.618\).
\(\omega_1=0.618\sqrt{k/m}\) with \(\boldsymbol\phi_1=[0.618,\ 1]^T\) (both floors move together) and \(\omega_2=1.618\sqrt{k/m}\) with \(\boldsymbol\phi_2=[-1.618,\ 1]^T\) (floors move in opposite directions).

Mode shapes are only defined up to a scale factor. A common convention is to normalise the roof displacement to 1.

08Modal superposition and damping modelsAdvanced18 min

Write the response as a sum of modes, \(\mathbf u(t)=\sum_n\boldsymbol\phi_n q_n(t)\). Orthogonality turns the \(N\) coupled equations into \(N\) independent SDOF equations. For ground shaking:

$$\ddot q_n+2\xi_n\omega_n\dot q_n+\omega_n^2q_n=-\Gamma_n\ddot u_g,\qquad \Gamma_n=\frac{\boldsymbol\phi_n^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\iota}{\boldsymbol\phi_n^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_n}$$

where \(\boldsymbol\iota\) is the influence vector (all ones for a shear building under horizontal ground motion). \(\Gamma_n\) is the modal participation factor. The effective modal mass \(M_n^{*}=(\boldsymbol\phi_n^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\iota)^2/(\boldsymbol\phi_n^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_n)\) tells you how much of the total mass each mode mobilises. The ratios over all modes add up to 100%, and design codes typically require enough modes to capture at least 90%.

Modal peaks do not occur at the same instant, so peak responses are combined with SRSS (\(\sqrt{\sum r_n^2}\)) or, when frequencies are close, the CQC rule.

Damping: Rayleigh model

Damping matrices are rarely derived from first principles. A practical choice is Rayleigh damping \(\mathbf C=a_0\mathbf M+a_1\mathbf K\), which is diagonalised by the same modes with

$$\xi_n=\frac{a_0}{2\omega_n}+\frac{a_1\omega_n}{2},\qquad a_0=\frac{2\xi\,\omega_i\omega_j}{\omega_i+\omega_j},\quad a_1=\frac{2\xi}{\omega_i+\omega_j}$$

The last two formulas force \(\xi_i=\xi_j=\xi\) at two chosen frequencies.

ExampleTarget 5% damping at \(\omega_i=10\) and \(\omega_j=30\) rad/s. Then \(a_0=2(0.05)(300)/40=0.75\) s⁻¹ and \(a_1=0.1/40=0.0025\) s. At \(\omega=20\) rad/s: \(\xi=0.75/40+0.0025\times10=4.4\%\), a little below target between the two anchor points, and rising rapidly outside them.

09Continuous systems: beam vibrationAdvanced16 min

A beam has mass spread along its length, so it has infinitely many DOF. For an Euler–Bernoulli beam with constant \(EI\) and mass per unit length \(\bar m\), free vibration obeys

$$EI\frac{\partial^4y}{\partial x^4}+\bar m\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}=0$$

Separating variables, \(y(x,t)=\psi(x)q(t)\), turns this into an eigenvalue problem whose solutions depend on the boundary conditions. For a simply supported beam of length \(L\) the modes are sine waves and

$$\omega_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2\sqrt{\frac{EI}{\bar m}},\qquad \psi_n(x)=\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$$

Frequencies grow with \(n^2\), so \(\omega_2=4\omega_1\) and \(\omega_3=9\omega_1\). For a cantilever, \(\omega_1=3.516\sqrt{EI/(\bar mL^4)}\) and \(\omega_2=22.03\sqrt{EI/(\bar mL^4)}\).

ExampleA simply supported span with \(L=10\) m, \(EI=2\times10^8\) N·m² and \(\bar m=1000\) kg/m has \(\omega_1=(\pi/10)^2\sqrt{2\times10^8/1000}=0.0987\times447.2=44.1\) rad/s, i.e. \(f_1=7.03\) Hz. Walking pace is about 2 Hz, so the first harmonic of footfall is far from resonance, but the 3rd harmonic (about 6 Hz) is getting close.

Rayleigh's method

For a quick estimate when the exact mode is unknown, assume a sensible shape \(\psi(x)\) satisfying the boundary conditions:

$$\omega^2\approx\frac{\int_0^L EI\,(\psi'')^2\,dx}{\int_0^L\bar m\,\psi^2\,dx}$$

The estimate is always an upper bound for \(\omega_1^2\), and it is remarkably accurate if \(\psi\) is close to the true shape (such as the static deflected shape).
